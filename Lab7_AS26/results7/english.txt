Dynamic programming and greedy algorithms are two of the most useful ideas in computer science. A greedy algorithm builds a solution one step at a time and always takes the choice that looks best right now. When the problem has the greedy-choice property, this simple strategy is also optimal, and the resulting program is short, fast and easy to reason about. Huffman coding is a famous example. Given the frequency of every character in a file, it repeatedly merges the two rarest symbols into a new tree, so that frequent characters end up near the root with short codes while rare characters sink deeper and receive longer codes. The result is a prefix code: no code word is the beginning of another, which means the encoded bits can be read from left to right without any separators.

Students often ask why we do not simply give every letter eight bits. The answer is that real text is very uneven. In English the letter e appears far more often than the letter z, and the space character appears more often than any letter. A fixed-length code wastes bits on the common symbols. A variable length code spends fewer bits where it matters most and therefore shrinks the file, often to about half of its original size. The same idea powers many real compression formats, usually combined with other techniques that find repeated phrases before the final Huffman step.

Of course, a greedy rule is not always correct. Choosing the activity that starts first, or the one that is shortest, can easily miss the best schedule. Choosing the item with the best value per kilo works when items can be split, but may fail when they cannot. Part of the craft is learning to prove when the greedy choice is safe and to find a small counterexample when it is not.
